How we help

Every person starts with a thorough assessment, followed by therapy tailored to their needs and goals. We work with babies and toddlers, children, teens and adults.

Speech

Helping children and adults speak more clearly and confidently

  • Speech sound delays and disorders
  • Apraxia of Speech
  • Stuttering and fluency

Language

Supporting understanding and use of language for everyday life and learning

  • Language delays and disorders in children
  • Adult language difficulties after stroke, head injury or illness
  • Social communication

Literacy

Building the skills needed to read, write and succeed at school

  • Reading and comprehension
  • Spelling
  • Written expression

Voice

Supporting clearer, stronger and more comfortable voices

  • Voice disorders
  • Vocal strain or fatigue
  • Voice rehabilitation following laryngectomy
  • Voice changes associated with neurological conditions

Swallowing

Supporting safe, comfortable swallowing for better health and quality of life

  • Swallowing difficulties following stroke (dysphagia)
  • Paediatric feeding and swallowing difficulties
  • Post-surgical swallowing rehabilitation

Home › For parents

Is your child's speech on track?

If you're worried about your child's speech and language development, you may have heard the advice to "wait and see", but waiting isn't always the best approach. Early identification of speech and language difficulties allows children to access the support they need when it matters most. If you have concerns, don't hesitate to seek advice.

By 4 years oldchildren should be understood by both familiar and unfamiliar listeners most of the time.

If people outside your family often struggle to understand your child's speech, it may be worth having their speech assessed.

Signs to look out for, by age

At 1 year old

  • Not yet saying first words
  • Not yet using gestures like pointing or waving
  • Only makes a few sounds

At 2 years old

  • Not yet following one step instructions
  • Not yet putting two words together
  • Not yet naming familiar objects when asked

At 3 years old

  • Not yet following two step instructions
  • Not yet asking questions
  • Not yet using simple three word sentences

At 4 to 5 years old

  • Not yet able to follow instructions at school
  • Not yet speaking in sentences
  • Not yet understood by strangers
  • Leaves out sounds or uses immature sounds, like "tat" for "cat"
  • Gets frustrated when trying to communicate

School aged children

Some children develop basic communication skills but struggle with the academic language they need once they reach school. Look out for difficulty with:

  • Following instructions in class
  • Listening or reading comprehension
  • Reading or spelling
  • Spoken or written language

Helpful reads for parents

Short guides based on the questions families ask us most.

Why "wait and see" isn't good advice

Some children catch up on their own, but many don't.

Read more

It's common to hear "they'll grow out of it". Some children do catch up naturally, but many do not, and it's hard to tell which group your child is in.

Early intervention can make a big difference to communication development, and it reduces frustration for both children and parents. An assessment either gives you reassurance or shows exactly what support is needed.

Speech therapy for school aged children

When everyday communication seems fine, but learning becomes a challenge.

Read more

Sometimes children develop basic communication skills, but once they reach school they struggle with the academic language they need to succeed.

These difficulties may affect a child's ability to follow classroom instructions, understand and answer questions, develop reading comprehension, spell, write and express their ideas clearly.

We provide comprehensive assessments and individualised therapy to support the language, literacy and communication skills children need to participate, learn and thrive at school.

How clear should my child's speech be?

A simple rule of thumb for 4 year olds.

Read more

By 4 years old, children should generally be understood by familiar and unfamiliar listeners most of the time, even though some speech sounds may still be developing.

Signs to look out for: people frequently struggle to understand your child, your child leaves out or changes sounds in words, uses immature speech patterns (for example saying "tat" instead of "cat"), becomes frustrated when trying to communicate, or their speech clarity doesn't seem to be improving over time.

Will learning two languages confuse my child?

Understanding bilingual language development and why your home language matters.

Read more

Growing up with more than one language is a wonderful gift. Many parents worry that learning two (or more) languages may confuse their child or delay speech and language development. However, learning more than one language does not cause speech or language delays.

Your home language is the foundation for communication, connection, culture and family relationships. Strong language skills in a child's first language support the development of additional languages.

You should never give up your home language to help your child learn another. Give your child the gift of both!
